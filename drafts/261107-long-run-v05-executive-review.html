Build the first, smallest stage of v0.5 now. It is called level 1a. The larger level 1b waits until 1a shows it is needed. The owner delegated that call to the operator on 2026-11-15, and the operator has adopted it.

Long-run runs AI sessions for hours or days to plan, build, check and release work. The owner is the human who sets goals and makes the calls. The operator is the AI assistant that runs the harness for the owner. The previous version, v0.4, reworked far longer than it built: one product took 5.3 hours to build and 34 hours to rework. v0.5 is a redesign around one small loop, and no part of it is built yet.

Build level 1a now; hold 1b until 1a shows the need

Level 1a is the smallest build that can run the next real experiment. It runs one bounded goal with one builder (the “flat” path) inside the product’s workspace. It then runs the same goal split into pieces on v0.4, to compare.

Built in 1a Waits for 1b
Frozen plan and checklist Splitting into pieces
One builder, one reviewer Parallel merging
The decider’s release Full stuck-run alarms
The workspace core Order and assumption checks
Code checks on artifacts Auditor’s judgment checks
Time and money stops Lessons for builders

A bounded goal is one with a clear finish, such as one product release. The decider is a separate AI that never builds and makes the binding calls, including the release. The workspace core gives every worker the product’s rules and its hooks, the small programs that guard what a session may do.

The kill rule. If the flat run wins on tokens and wall time at an equal quality score, level 1b waits. One earlier whole-goal test favours the flat path: a single session rebuilt a v0.4 goal in about 13 minutes against 2.2 hours, on about an eighth of the tokens. That session graded itself and was one sample.

Today’s tests add three changes to 1a. The git rule goes into the workers’ rules first, because it is the one rule measured to work. A code check on artifacts catches a money row with zero units unless a reason is given. Only the judgment roles get operator lessons in 1a, since builders’ lessons are not yet tagged for selection.

One loop runs inside each product’s own workspace

Every piece of work runs the same loop: plan, build, check, try, decide. A piece too big for one builder splits, and each smaller piece runs the same loop. Every worker runs inside its product’s workspace. After the kickoff, the system checks quality itself, and nothing waits on the owner’s signature.

Part What it does
Product workspace Holds each product’s rules and history
Workspace layer Gives every worker the rules
Context auditor Checks facts exist and are findable
Order check Stops work starting too early
Watchdog Re-plans stuck pieces, then escalates
Two clocks Keeps open goals from waiting

The product workspace. Each product gets one folder, or its own code repository when it has one. Its runs live inside that folder, so a later run inherits the lessons and decisions of earlier ones. v0.4 workers got none of the workspace’s rules or memory, and that gap is what the layer closes.

The context auditor. It checks that every fact the plan cites exists in the workspace. It also checks that a fresh session can reach a sample of those facts through the indexes alone. A failure becomes a fix task for whoever owns the missing note. A second failure on the same check goes to the decider, never to the owner.

The order check. Code holds a table of what must exist before each kind of work, such as no paid ads before a product listing and payment. It adds any missing dependency and refuses a plan that loops back on itself. In simulation it cut rework from 14.7 to 2.6 sessions per run and removed early money tasks, for a 6% longer schedule.

Two clocks for open-ended goals. A business never finishes, and the market can take weeks to respond. The build clock reads every 30 minutes and asks only whether pieces are landing. The market clock reads weekly and asks whether revenue tracks the plan. Market data updates the priorities and never holds work back.

Replays show one working rule and a cheap layer

The tests replayed real v0.4 decisions with and without the workspace layer. Each case is a recorded error, rerun many times in a fresh session. “Right” means the session avoided the original error.

The git rule fixed a lost-file commit in 6 of 6 runs

The case was a commit that silently dropped a file the work needed. The git-discipline block of the rules says to respect the ignore list and check what is staged right before committing. With that block alone, 6 of 6 runs were right. Without it, 1 of 6 was. The verification rules alone were right in 0 of 6 runs.

General rules and a written lesson missed a data-meaning error

The case was a billing row that showed $311,904 to act on against 0 rides. The rules contain a line saying missing data is not zero, and one test put the exact lesson in front of the worker. Both scored 0 of 10. So level 1a adds a code check on artifacts for this class of error. A third arm, giving the lesson at the moment the worker writes the file, is still running.

The layer adds about a tenth to a quarter to cost

Measure Added by the layer
Peak context 7,600 to 9,800 tokens
Billed cost 1.09x to 1.26x
Wall time 1.12x to 1.17x
Off-topic reads No rise

In a simulated run the size of the largest v0.4 build, the layer broke no budget. Wall time rose 12% to 19% and usage 14% to 39%, counting no benefit from fewer errors.

A stuck run gets a local re-plan within 3 hours

The watchdog first re-plans a stuck piece in code, without waking the decider. On 4 of 5 failing v0.4 runs, that first re-plan came at 1.2 to 2.2 active hours, and it never fired on a healthy run. The fifth run, the billing reconciliation that ran 63 hours, gets its re-plan at 4.0 hours. Separately, time and cost alarms wake the decider at 3.6 to 5.9 active hours on four of the five runs, including 3.6 on the 63-hour run.

The owner’s target was to catch a run that is not converging within 3 hours. The operator adopted the local re-plan as meeting it (open question Q-7, now closed). No tested alarm wakes the decider within 3 hours without also waking it on healthy runs.

The new open-goal rules held in simulation

On a simulated 12-week business, draft 10 would have raised about 16 false alarms a week while waiting for market data. With the two clocks, 180 simulated runs raised none. An injected 24-hour stall was caught at 2 hours in 30 of 30 runs. A failing strategy was caught at a median of 4 weeks, with 1 false alarm in 120 healthy runs.

The endurance replay showed the resume mechanics work: a fresh session picked up from the progress file, did the next step, and redid no committed work, at about a third of a full session’s cost. Three other replay cases could not test the layer, because the bare worker did not reproduce the original error or the original right answer. None showed significant harm.

Calls still open with the owner, none blocking 1a. These are the six design questions Q-1 to Q-6, three review findings, two gaps from held-out cases, how to read the no-regression result, and whether to rename the builder role.

What changed since draft 10

Change since draft 10 Why

Alarms refit Draft 10’s held at one timing
3-hour target at re-plan No alarm met it cleanly
Level 1 split, 1a and 1b Build only what is needed

The workspace, auditor, order check and two clocks come from the owner’s five rulings for draft 11. Draft 10’s alarm claims held only when the watchdog’s 30-minute check started at one particular minute. Refit over every start minute, the alarms stay quiet on healthy runs, and the decider wakes later. That is why the 3-hour target now rests on the local re-plan. The review found four must-fix and nine should-fix problems, and draft 11 fixes all thirteen. Of the review’s suggested cuts, it moves some to level 1b.
