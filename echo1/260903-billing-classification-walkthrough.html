How a ride gets classified for billing

The new process, in plain language: what is changing and why, the new fields, and the process from submission to billing. Click any diagram to open it full-screen with zoom and pan.

Reviewed with the team 2026-08-28 Process v0.7.0 Updated 2026-09-03

What is changing, and why

Two things changed outside the app this year, and the app’s old rules stopped being true.

The state changed what a crisis ride is. Until this spring, Crisis and Secured meant the same thing in the app: if a ride went in a secured vehicle, it was billed as crisis. The state’s criteria now require documentation — an LMHP evaluation, an EOD, a third-party affidavit or a court order — for a ride to be a crisis ride. So a secured ride is no longer automatically a crisis ride, and a crisis ride is only billable as one when that paperwork exists. We have to decide “is this a crisis ride” on the evidence, before the ride goes out, not from the vehicle it went in.

The state ended prescheduled rides under the old arrangement. In their place we now have a set of individual contracts — ODMHSAS facilities, Oceans, the county and out-of-state contracts — each with its own payment terms. A non-crisis ride has to be billed to a specific contract, and nothing on a request says on its own which one. A prescheduled ride that isn’t covered by a contract isn’t approved at all.

So going forward both questions are answered at the front of the process, when the request comes in:

  1. What kind of ride is this? Crisis, Contracted, Self-Funded, or a Reject. The system proposes an answer from what’s on the form; a dispatcher or eligibility checker confirms it.
  2. Who exactly gets the bill? For a crisis ride, the Medicaid payer the eligibility check finds (the state Medicaid agency or the member’s MCO). For a contract ride, the contract that owns the form it arrived on.

Three things make this work:

  • Every contract group uses its own dedicated form. The form tells us who’s responsible. A contract ride sent through the website is sent back to be resubmitted on the right link.
  • Crisis paperwork is chased to a conclusion. A crisis ride that goes out before its paperwork arrives is tracked, chased at three levels, and ends up either confirmed and billed or written off — never left in limbo. From 2026-10-01 the new state contract lets the state demand that paperwork before paying, so this stops being optional.
  • Every decision is recorded with its reason, so “why did this bill to X?” always has an answer.

The new fields, briefly

Field What it means Who sets it
Billing Class One of four: Crisis / Contracted / Self-Funded (DRC) / Reject. There is deliberately no “not sure”. System proposes; dispatcher / eligibility checker confirms
Crisis Confirmation Status For crisis rides only: Confirmed (paperwork in hand) / Provisional (went out on a manager’s approval, paperwork still owed) / Mgr Review (escalated) / Documentation Failed (chased, never obtained) / N/A Dispatcher, manager, processing
Insurance (as submitted) The insurance and policy number the person filling out the form told us. Recorded as given. The submitter
Payer Who we actually bill. This is the field billing runs on. Eligibility check sets it; billing confirms it

Two things that are not billing fields, so they don’t change: Secured / Unsecured (how we route the vehicle — every crisis ride is secured, but not every secured ride is a crisis) and Prescheduled (is this for now or for later — contract rides tend to be prescheduled and Medicaid crisis rides tend not to be, but that’s a tendency, not a rule).

The process, from submission to billing

1A request comes in

Two kinds of form. A contract group’s dedicated form can be used for either a crisis ride or a prescheduled ride — the group submits everything through their one link. The website form is crisis-only.

The submitter tells us the client’s insurance and policy number. If it’s a crisis, they upload the 43a paperwork (an EOD, an LMHP statement, a third-party affidavit or a court order) or tick the crisis attestation. The attestation’s wording is being strengthened so it reads as a formal 43a statement, but it’s a backstop for when a document can’t be obtained, never the sole basis for a crisis ride.

The system then proposes a classification, in this order:

  • 43a evidence present or attested → propose Crisis. Crisis outranks the form: a crisis ride on a contract form is still a crisis ride.
  • Otherwise, arrived on a contract group’s dedicated form → propose Contracted, with that contract as the payer.
  • Otherwise (website or general form, no crisis) → propose Reject and bounce it.

Self-Funded is never proposed by the system; a person decides that.

2Two checks, at the same time, by different people

Nothing goes out until both say yes.

  • Eligibility check — will we get paid? The dispatcher or eligibility checker sees the system’s proposal and the reasons behind it, runs the coverage lookup, and confirms the Billing Class and the payer. This is where the classification becomes final. Section 3 walks through it.
  • QA check — can we run this ride? Right address, right time, right vehicle, real person on the other end. Nothing to do with billing.

When both are done the request reads as Ready and can be dispatched.

3The eligibility check

This is the amber box on the main chart, and it’s where both questions from the top of this document get answered: what kind of ride is this, and who gets the bill. Three parts: the dispatcher or eligibility checker decides which kind of ride it is; if they can’t, a manager does; and the kind of ride decides who gets the bill.

Which kind of ride

  • Crisis — the 43a evidence is there, or the crisis signals are strong and the paperwork is on its way. Needs a Crisis Confirmation Status and a Medicaid payer.
  • Contracted — arrived on the contract’s dedicated form. The payer is that contract. (A manager with the designated permission, or above, may classify one by hand as an exception — for instance a contract ride that came in on the website. That’s recorded as an exception, not the normal path.)
  • Self-Funded (DRC) — no crisis, no contract, but a veteran or a child, and RideCare chooses to serve. A manager has to approve it.
  • Reject — none of the above, including a contract ride sent through the website. It’s rejected with a note to resubmit on the group’s own link. Front-line staff don’t fix it for them; only a manager with the designated permission can classify it as a contract ride.

When the dispatcher can’t decide

They escalate it to Mgr Review — a manager-owned item that expects a prompt decision, not a holding pile. The approver must be the operations manager (Scott) or above. The manager lands it on one of the four answers. If it’s a crisis whose paperwork isn’t in hand yet, the manager can approve it provisionally: the ride goes out, and it’s flagged so processing knows to chase the paperwork afterwards.

Who gets the bill

The Billing Class tells us which list to look in; one more step picks the name:

  • Crisis → the insurance eligibility check. The submitted coverage is checked against the state’s Medicaid eligibility portal, and the payer is whatever it returns: the state Medicaid agency itself, or the managed care organization (MCO) the member is enrolled with. Today a crisis ride is never billed to private insurance. Colorado works the same way: its portal tells us which Regional Accountable Entity the member’s primary care provider is assigned to, and that’s the payer. If the check finds no coverage, the ride still runs as a crisis ride and billing works its existing process: it tries to obtain coverage for the client through the state’s payer-of-last-resort programs.
  • Contracted → the contract that owns the form. Submitting on a group’s own link is that group taking responsibility for the ride. Each form is its own tracked entity for billing. Where several forms bill to one party they share a billing group, and their invoices go to that party — ODMHSAS is the main example: several forms, one billing group.
  • Self-Funded → RideCare (DRC).
  • Reject → nobody.

Every proposal and every confirmation is recorded with what it was based on. There’s no “confidence score” — just the facts that led to the answer, and if a person changed it by hand, who and why.

4Ready → dispatched → the ride

Once both checks are done the ride is dispatched and run as usual. Nothing about the classification changes during the ride.

5After the ride: processing and the paperwork chase

For most rides, processing certifies the record and releases it to billing.

For a crisis ride that went out provisionally, processing works from a searchable list of every such ride and chases the paperwork through three levels before anything is written off:

  1. Processing — the driver got the document during the ride, or dispatch obtains it from the submitter.
  2. The eligibility specialist — follows up directly with the case worker or referring agency.
  3. Final review (Jeff) — the last look before a write-off. The document turns up, or an exception is approved and recorded, or it’s marked Documentation Failed and written off as non-billable.

Either way the ride reaches an end state and is certified. This matters more from 2026-10-01, when the new state contract lets the state demand the 43a documents before paying.

6Billing

Billing confirms the payer, makes any final corrections, and sends the ride out on the right path: 837P claim for a Medicaid agency or MCO, invoice for a contract payer. What the submitter originally told us about the client’s insurance stays on the record untouched.
