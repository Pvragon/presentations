How a ride gets classified for billing

The new process, in plain language: what is changing and why, the new fields, and the process from submission to billing.

Reviewed with the team 2026-08-28Process v0.7.0Updated 2026-09-03

At a glance

Every ride is classified for billing when the request comes in, not after the ride.

  1. The system proposes a Billing Class from the form. Crisis if 43a evidence is attached or attested; Contracted if it arrived on a contract group’s dedicated form; otherwise Reject. Self-Funded (DRC) is only ever set by a person.
  2. Two checks run in parallel before dispatch. Eligibility (will we get paid?) confirms the class and the payer: a Medicaid agency or MCO for crisis, the form’s contract for contracted. QA (can we run it?) checks the ride itself. Both yes means Ready.
  3. Can’t decide? Mgr Review. The operations manager or above lands it, and may send a crisis ride out provisionally with paperwork still owed.
  4. After the ride, provisional crisis rides are chased at three levels, processing then the eligibility specialist then Jeff, and end confirmed or written off, never in limbo.
  5. Billing confirms the payer and sends an 837P claim (Medicaid or MCO) or an invoice (contract). Every decision is recorded with its reason.

What is changing, and why

Two things changed outside the app this year, and the app’s old rules stopped being true.

The state changed what a crisis ride is. Until this spring, Crisis and Secured meant the same thing in the app: a ride in a secured vehicle was billed as crisis. The state now requires documentation (an LMHP evaluation, an EOD, a third-party affidavit or a court order) for a ride to count as crisis. So a secured ride is no longer automatically a crisis ride: we decide on the evidence, before the ride goes out, not from the vehicle it went in.

The state ended prescheduled rides under the old arrangement. In their place we have individual contracts (ODMHSAS facilities, Oceans, the county, out-of-state contracts), each with its own payment terms. A non-crisis ride has to be billed to a specific contract, and nothing on a request says which one. A prescheduled ride no contract covers isn’t approved at all.

The new fields, briefly

Field What it means Who sets it
Billing Class One of four: Crisis / Contracted / Self-Funded (DRC) / Reject. There is deliberately no “not sure”. System proposes; dispatcher / eligibility checker confirms
Crisis Confirmation Status For crisis rides only: Confirmed (paperwork in hand) / Provisional (went out on a manager’s approval, paperwork still owed) / Mgr Review (escalated) / Documentation Failed (chased, never obtained) / N/A Dispatcher, manager, processing
Insurance (as submitted) The insurance and policy number the person filling out the form told us. Recorded as given. The submitter
Payer Who we actually bill. This is the field billing runs on. Eligibility check sets it; billing confirms it

Two fields are not billing fields, so they don’t change. Secured / Unsecured is how we route the vehicle: every crisis ride is secured, but not every secured ride is a crisis. Prescheduled is whether the ride is for now or later: contract rides tend to be prescheduled and Medicaid crisis rides tend not to be, but that’s a tendency, not a rule.

The process, from submission to billing

1A request comes in

Two kinds of form. A contract group’s dedicated form takes either a crisis ride or a prescheduled ride; the group submits everything through their one link. The website form is crisis-only.

The submitter tells us the client’s insurance and policy number. If it’s a crisis, they upload the 43a paperwork or tick the crisis attestation. The attestation’s wording is being strengthened toward a formal 43a statement, but it is a backstop for when a document can’t be obtained, never the sole basis for a crisis ride.

The system then proposes a classification, in this order:

  • 43a evidence present or attested → propose Crisis. Crisis outranks the form: a crisis ride on a contract form is still a crisis ride.
  • Otherwise, arrived on a contract group’s dedicated form → propose Contracted, with that contract as the payer.
  • Otherwise (website or general form, no crisis) → propose Reject and bounce it.

Self-Funded is never proposed by the system; a person decides that.

2Two checks, at the same time, by different people

Nothing goes out until both say yes.

  • Eligibility check: will we get paid? The dispatcher or eligibility checker sees the system’s proposal and its reasons, runs the coverage lookup, and confirms the Billing Class and the payer. This is where the classification becomes final. Section 3 walks through it.
  • QA check: can we run this ride? Right address, right time, right vehicle, real person on the other end. Nothing to do with billing.

When both are done the request reads as Ready and can be dispatched.

3The eligibility check

This is the amber box on the main chart, where the two questions get answered: what kind of ride is this, and who gets the bill.

Which kind of ride

  • Crisis — the 43a evidence is there, or the crisis signals are strong and the paperwork is on its way. Needs a Crisis Confirmation Status and a Medicaid payer.
  • Contracted — arrived on the contract’s dedicated form. The payer is that contract.
  • Self-Funded (DRC) — no crisis, no contract, but a veteran or a child, and RideCare chooses to serve. A manager has to approve it.
  • Reject — none of the above, including a contract ride sent through the website. It goes back with a note to resubmit on the group’s own link. Only a manager with the designated permission, or above, may instead classify it as a contract ride, and that is recorded as an exception.

When the dispatcher can’t decide

They escalate it to Mgr Review, a manager-owned item that expects a prompt decision, not a holding pile. The approver must be the operations manager (Scott) or above, and lands it on one of the four answers. If it’s a crisis whose paperwork isn’t in hand yet, the manager can approve it provisionally: the ride goes out, flagged so processing knows to chase the paperwork afterwards.

Who gets the bill

The Billing Class tells us which list to look in; one more step picks the name:

  • Crisis → the insurance eligibility check. The submitted coverage is checked against the state’s Medicaid eligibility portal; the payer is whatever it returns, the state Medicaid agency or the member’s managed care organization (MCO). Crisis rides are never billed to private insurance today. In Colorado the portal names the Regional Accountable Entity of the member’s primary care provider, and that’s the payer. If the check finds no coverage, the ride still runs and billing seeks coverage through the state’s payer-of-last-resort programs.
  • Contracted → the contract that owns the form: submitting on a group’s own link is that group taking responsibility for the ride. Each form is its own billing entity; forms that bill to one party share a billing group (ODMHSAS is the main example).
  • Self-Funded → RideCare (DRC).
  • Reject → nobody.

Every proposal and confirmation is recorded with what it was based on. There’s no “confidence score”, just the facts that led to the answer and, if a person changed it by hand, who and why.

4Ready → dispatched → the ride

Once both checks are done the ride is dispatched and run as usual. Nothing about the classification changes during the ride.

5After the ride: processing and the paperwork chase

For most rides, processing certifies the record and releases it to billing.

For a crisis ride that went out provisionally, processing works from a searchable list of every such ride and chases the paperwork through three levels before anything is written off:

  1. Processing — the driver got the document during the ride, or dispatch obtains it from the submitter.
  2. The eligibility specialist — follows up directly with the case worker or referring agency.
  3. Final review (Jeff) — the last look before a write-off. The document turns up, or an exception is approved and recorded, or it’s marked Documentation Failed and written off as non-billable.

Either way the ride reaches an end state and is certified. This matters more from 2026-10-01, when the new state contract lets the state demand the 43a documents before paying.

6Billing

Billing confirms the payer, makes any final corrections, and sends the ride out on the right path: 837P claim for a Medicaid agency or MCO, invoice for a contract payer. What the submitter originally told us about the client’s insurance stays on the record untouched.
