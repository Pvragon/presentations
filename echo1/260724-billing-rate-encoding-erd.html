Payer configuration: requirements and proposed design

The capabilities any payer and tenant configuration must have to bill every tenant's contracts in any US state. The proposed design uses one dated schedule per payer, rate lines for every priced term, billing identities chosen per payer, and a record on every claim of the settings that built it.

1. A claim is built from the transport and four configuration areas

Apart from the transport itself, each area is held in its own tables:

  • Pricing: the payer’s rate schedule, dated by service date, and its rate lines: what is priced, at what rate, and under which procedure codes and modifiers.
  • Claim settings: the payer’s submission configuration, dated by service date, which sets how its claims are built and billed. It inherits a claim profile, a shared template such as a state’s 837 rules, and names the billing identity.
  • Routing: the payer’s routing configuration, dated by send date, which sets where its claim files go. It points at the tenant’s clearinghouse account.
  • Billing identity: a legal business the payer pays, with its name, NPI (national provider number) and tax ID. A tenant has a dated default and can bill under more than one identity at a time, because payers recognize a new NPI at different times.

Every transport is billed to exactly one payer, decided before billing starts. A claim groups transports, records every setting that built it, and is sent as an 837P or an invoice.

2. Any design must meet fifteen requirements

Any payer and tenant configuration must meet these fifteen requirements to bill every tenant’s contracts in any US state. Echo1 decides how to implement them. Capabilities listed as future features are needed by no payer yet, but each must be addable without restructuring. Until one is built, configuration that needs it is refused.

2.1 The whole system is configured, isolated, dated, safe, and reproducible

R1. System: configuration, not code. Any tenant sets up any payer through configuration. A new behavior is a new option every tenant can use, never per-payer code.

R2. System: tenant isolation. Each tenant’s data is isolated in the database. A remittance or status response is matched to a claim within its tenant, never by claim number alone.

R3. System: dated versions. The system must support:

  • service dates taken in the transport’s local timezone;
  • pricing and claim settings resolved on the service date, and routing on the send date;
  • changes to a version in use only by a new version, with no gaps;
  • versions used by a billed claim locked against edits;
  • a retroactive version, one effective before claims already billed, such as a rate or billing identity change, entered by a user who is both a tenant admin and a billing admin, and applied to every unbilled transport from its effective date;
  • before it is saved, a warning listing every claim and invoice billed on or after its effective date;
  • on each of those, a billing note saying it was billed under the earlier version and that the new version was entered later with an effective date before that billing; and a list of them for the billing team to review and correct.

R4. System: safe pricing. The system must:

  • give exactly one price per transport, refusing conflicting settings on save;
  • stop with a reason when data is missing, never pricing it as zero;
  • show the user every transport a billing run skips or stops, with the reason, so none drops out of billing unseen;
  • price only on the payer and measured facts of the transport, with who pays decided upstream.

R5. System: reproducible claims. Each claim records the configuration, identity and address that built it and how each charge was derived, and reproduces exactly from that record.

2.2 The platform keeps shared templates once

R6. Platform: shared templates. The system must support:

  • rules common to many payers kept once, as a dated template each payer references (a state’s 837 rules);
  • a payer overriding one setting of its template without copying the rest;
  • starting templates for rate cards, copied into a payer and then edited;
  • invoice layouts kept once and named by each payer that prints with them;
  • templates kept by the platform for every tenant, and by a tenant for its own payers.

2.3 Each tenant holds its billing identities and settings

R7. Tenant: billing identities. The system must support:

  • more than one billing identity per tenant in use at once: legal name, NPI, tax ID, taxonomy, address;
  • a dated tenant default identity;
  • a new NPI or tax ID as a new identity, and dated address and contact changes on the same one.

R8. Tenant: settings. The system must support:

  • a home timezone, the default timezone in which each payer’s caps and periods are counted;
  • a claim number (CLM01) on every claim, unique within each clearinghouse account it is sent through, in a form payers do not reformat, and returned on the payment;
  • clearinghouse connections: the tenant’s submitter id and the settings that send claim files to a clearinghouse and fetch its responses.

4.3 Payer settings belong to the payer, and identities to the tenant

5. The payer and the service date decide the billing identity

A claim’s billing identity is decided by its payer and its service date, never by the transport. This matters now because RideCare is moving from David Roberts Consulting LLC (trading as OK Ride Care) to Ride Care Inc., a new entity with its own NPI, payer by payer as each recognizes the new one.

  • A transport carries its payer and no billing identity.
  • The payer’s submission configuration in effect on the service date names an identity, or is blank and follows the tenant default in effect on that date.
  • A claim covers one payer, covered client and service date, and records the identity.
  • A payer-assigned provider number, a taxonomy override or a contract code describes one identity’s enrollment, so a configuration that sets one must name its identity.
  • A new address or phone is a new dated version of the identity, and no payer configuration changes.

6. Every setting change is a new dated version

Every dated setting changes by adding a version, and the editor asks for one date: the day the version takes effect.

6.1 A new version ends the one before it, with no gaps

  • New versions. A new version ends the one before it on the day before its own start. A version entered between two others ends the day before the later one.
  • Gaps. A version may be followed by a gap only when it was ended on purpose: one configuration ended, or the payer was deactivated.
  • Missing versions. When no version is in effect on the service date, the transport is not billed and the missing setting is named for review.
  • Unused versions. A version no billed claim or sent invoice uses may be edited or deleted. Billed means a claim that has left draft, or an invoice that has been sent. A used version is never edited or deleted.
  • Fixing a wrong version. A new version from the same start date replaces the wrong one in full. The old one is kept, because billed claims point to it, and is never used again.

6.2 A retroactive version flags what was already billed and never changes it

A retroactive version takes effect before claims already billed under the version before it. Only a user who is both a tenant admin and a billing admin may enter one. Before saving, the user sees every billed claim and sent invoice it reaches, and confirms. On saving:

  • transports not yet billed take the new version, and draft claims on the old one are rebuilt;
  • billed claims and sent invoices keep what they were billed under; each is flagged for billing review and gets a billing note, for example: “Billed under rate schedule RS-OK, version effective 2026-01-01. A version effective 2026-03-01 was entered on 2026-04-10 by J. Smith, with an effective date before this claim was billed. Review whether this claim needs a correction.”;
  • every flagged claim and invoice appears on one list for the billing team, who decide any replacement, void, or rebill later. A biller can clear or set the flag, and every change of the flag adds a billing note.

6.3 Deactivating a payer ends its versions on a set date

Deactivating a payer ends the schedule and the submission configuration in effect on the payer’s last date, and deletes any later versions, which nothing billed can have used. It refuses a last date earlier than a billed claim’s service date.

Transport’s service date Result
up to deactivation prices and bills as before; routing stays in effect, so these claims can still be sent, replaced, and voided
after it not billed; flagged as after the payer’s deactivation
after reactivation priced on the new versions; dates between the deactivation and the new start stay a gap

Reactivation inserts new versions from a new start date. Deactivating one configuration alone, a schedule for example, ends that row as terminated.

7. A transport becomes a claim in five stages

Every setting is taken as in effect on the transport’s service date, except routing, which is taken on the send date. A transport that cannot pass a stage stops there and is flagged for review with the reason.

The first three stages decide every amount. The last two group, sum, and render.

7.1 Billing finds the payer’s settings in effect on the service date, or stops

Billing first finds the one rate schedule and the one submission configuration in effect on the transport’s service date, and stops if either is missing. A transport enters billing once it is processed and billing has confirmed its payer. Its service date is the scheduled pickup’s date, in the pickup location’s timezone.

Versions never overlap, so resolution never finds two. The billing identity follows from the payer and the service date (section 5). Who pays is decided before pricing: a rate line never tests the payer, program, crisis status, or requesting entity.

A transport is skipped when any of these holds. The billing run lists every skipped transport to the user, with its reason, so it can be fixed and billed:

  • its outcome is one the schedule does not pay, such as a Colorado cancellation;
  • it is already on a current claim;
  • the payer requires verified coverage and the transport’s coverage is not verified.

A cap is checked here too, against the payer’s claims in the cap’s period. Dispatchers see the budget remaining before a transport runs. When a cap is exceeded, billing warns and never blocks, because the transport has already happened.

7.2 Transports form priced units: client legs, vehicle trips, or periods

A priced unit is what one price is charged for. The schedule’s unit of work decides how transports become priced units.

  • Client leg. Each transport is one unit.
  • Vehicle trip. The transports one vehicle carried together are one trip. A transport is in the trip when its pickup is within 15 minutes and 100 meters of the trip’s first pickup, and its dropoff within 15 minutes and 100 meters of the first transport’s dropoff. These tests use the actual pickup and dropoff times, never the scheduled ones.
  • Round trip. When the schedule prices round trips as one, a trip and the vehicle’s next trip form one unit if the next trip brings at least one of the same clients back to the first pickup, on the same day or the next. A trip with no return is priced one way. A movement with no client aboard is a dead leg and is never a unit.
  • Period. One unit per period, created once the period’s last day has ended. It holds every transport dated in the period. It exists even with no transports, because a fixed fee is owed either way.

Every priced unit has one outcome: completed, canceled, or no-show. Each canceled or no-show transport is its own unit. A transport canceled before the vehicle departed is a void, which operations removes before billing. A transport recorded as canceled with no departure is therefore not priced, and is flagged for review. A vehicle trip carrying clients of more than one payer is split by payer, with a warning. On a draft claim, a user may merge or split vehicle trips, giving a reason.

7.3 Each unit’s price is the sum of its terms, raised to any minimum

A term is one rate line applied to one unit, with its quantity, rate, and charge.

The unit’s outcome and conditions choose which rate lines apply

Only the lines for the unit’s outcome are considered. Each line has one of four roles:

Role What it does Example
Single line a base charge or an add-on: applies when its condition holds, alongside every other line Oklahoma: $160 per transport
Alternatives exactly one line of the group applies, chosen by distance or destination, with an optional “otherwise” alternative Sedgwick: named routes, else mileage
Brackets every bracket applies, each to the part of one quantity inside it, with no free quantity none yet
Minimum raises the unit’s price to a floor, counting every term of its outcome; one per outcome Sedgwick: $85 per trip

Each term is measured, reduced, rounded, tested, and priced

  • Free quantity or threshold. A line has one or the other, never both. Free miles are subtracted from every unit. A threshold prices nothing at or below it, and then every mile.
  • Rounding before every test. A threshold, an alternative’s range, and a documentation trigger all read the rounded value. With half-up whole miles, 30.4 miles reads as 30 and is not over 30; 30.5 reads as 31 and bills 31 miles.
  • What is not rounded. A wait threshold reads whole minutes, before conversion. A condition on a count or on a published fuel price compares the value as recorded.
  • Brackets. The quantity is rounded once, then split across the brackets, so the parts add up to the rounded quantity.
  • Evidence. Each term records the measure as read, at full precision, beside the rounded quantity it billed.

approach from the nearest driven stop before the pickup, else the route’s starting location
loaded leg pickup to dropoff
return carried by the route’s last driven transport, when the route carries more than one transport or is the driver’s last route: to the ending location, else the starting location

A transport with another client’s stop between its pickup and dropoff, or with no route, is not priced on the vehicle path and is flagged for review.

A minimum raises the unit’s price to a floor

The unit’s price is the sum of its terms. When the unit has a minimum for its outcome and the sum is below it, the difference is one more term, billed under the minimum line’s own code. The minimum applies to each unit separately, so five Sedgwick trips are each raised to $85.

On the canceled outcome, a mileage line on the miles driven before the cancellation and a $45 minimum together price “$45 or the mileage driven, whichever is greater” (Sedgwick, Larned, and Wichita VA).

7.4 Units are grouped into claims by payer, client, and day, or as the unit of work sets

The unit of work decides which units share a claim:

Unit of work One claim per Client on the claim
Client leg payer, client, and service date the client
Vehicle trip payer and service date none
Period payer and period none

The billing identity is not part of the grouping. The payer and the service date decide it, so every unit on one claim shares one identity. The claim records the identity and the schedule, submission configuration, and claim profile versions that built it, so it can be reproduced exactly.

A late transport whose claim is still a draft is added, and the draft is rebuilt. When that claim has left draft, the transport is not added and is flagged for review. Adding it would be a replacement claim, which is claim operations.

7.5 Each claim is built into service lines and sent as an 837P or an invoice

Each rate line with a charge on the claim becomes service lines, as the rate line sets:

  • one line per claim: all of the claim’s terms of that rate line on one service line;
  • one line per trip: one service line per priced unit.

A service line carries the rate line’s procedure code, modifiers, and label, and one date of service. Its units and charge are exactly the sum of its terms, so a claim’s total always equals the sum of its priced units. A line with no units is left off.

  • Unit cap. A rate line with a unit cap fills lines up to the cap in unit order. One unit’s term may be split across lines; its parts add up to the unsplit charge.
  • Order. Lines run by earliest start, then by the rate line’s position, or in the order of services a payer requires.
  • Repeat modifiers. A later trip on the same day gets the rate line’s repeat modifier, such as Colorado’s 76. Trips are counted across the whole day, so the count holds when a claim is split. A line carries at most four modifiers.
  • Line limit. A claim over the payer’s maximum number of lines splits into more claims by whole units, so a transport is never split across claims. A single unit too big for one claim stops for review.

The claim is then sent through the payer’s channel:

Channel How it is sent
837P written from what the claim recorded
Invoice an invoice run, below
Portal an invoice run; the user submits it through the payer’s portal and marks it sent

An 837P takes routing and the identity’s name, address, and contact as in effect on the send date. An invoice run starts on demand: the user picks the payer and an as-of date, and the invoice covers every ready claim since the payer’s last invoice under the same billing identity. A pre-issue check lists transports that are missing, not ready, or late, and blocks nothing. A run that spans a change of billing identity or payer settings stops for review. The payer’s settings choose one invoice for the period or one per claim, rows per rate line or per priced unit, and the due date.
