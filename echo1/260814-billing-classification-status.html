How we decide who pays for a ride

Every ride we run has to end up on somebody's bill. This is the design we've landed on for working out whose bill that is — in plain words — followed by the questions we still have to answer.

Design summary Status as of 2026-08-14

02Question one — what kind of ride is this?

There are exactly four answers, and somebody has to pick one.

  • Crisis. A mental-health emergency. Someone official has said this person is in crisis, and we have the paperwork that proves it — or we're going to go get it.
  • Contracted. A planned ride for a group we've signed a contract with. They get an invoice.
  • We cover it ourselves. No emergency, no contract, but the rider is a veteran or a child and we decide to help anyway. A manager has to say yes.
  • No. None of the above. We turn the ride down.

There is no "not sure" box

We left it out on purpose. A "not sure" pile is a pile that never gets emptied, and every ride in it is a ride we can't bill. Someone picks one of the four, or the ride is a No.

Who decides, and when

A dispatcher decides, at the moment the ride is requested — not weeks later in billing. Two different people run two different checks at the same time:

  • Will we get paid? This is where the four answers above come from.
  • Can we actually run this ride? Right address, right time, right vehicle, real person on the other end. Nothing to do with money.

Both have to come back yes before the ride goes out the door.

What if the crisis paperwork isn't there yet?

We can still run the ride. We mark it as promised, and after the ride the driver or a dispatcher goes and gets the paper. If it turns up, we confirm it and bill it. If it never turns up, we mark it "couldn't prove it" and we absorb the cost. Either way it reaches an end — nothing sits half-finished forever.

What if the dispatcher can't tell?

They hand it to a manager, and the manager is expected to answer promptly. It becomes a named person's job rather than a queue nobody owns.

What if someone used the wrong form?

We send it back and ask them to resubmit on the right link. We don't quietly fix it for them — if we always clean up after people, the wrong forms never stop coming.

03Question two — who exactly gets the bill?

This is the half that's newly designed. It starts with a problem worth understanding, because the whole design is a response to it.

The problem: one box doing two jobs

There is a single box on the ride record that gets used twice for two different things. When the ride comes in, the person requesting it uses that box to tell us what coverage this rider has. Later, billing uses the very same box to record who we're actually invoicing — and types straight over the top.

The requester's original answer is simply gone. Nobody can go back and see what we were told, which means nobody can check whether billing's change was right.

The fix: two boxes instead of one

One box holds what the requester told us. It is never overwritten, ever. A second box holds who we decided to bill, and billing can still change that one when they need to. Both boxes already exist in the new system — we just have to start using them for one job each.

Then we pick the name

The answer to question one tells us which list to look in:

  • Crisis → look up the rider's insurance.
  • Contracted → work out which of our contracts it belongs to.
  • Any ride in Colorado → the county decides it; that's how the state works.
  • We cover it → the payer is us.

Working out which contract: clues, strongest first

For a contracted ride we go down a list of clues in order. The first clue that works is the answer.

  1. Which form did they use? A group with its own submission link is telling us plainly who they are.
  2. Are both ends of the ride places we hold a contract with?
  3. Is one end? If so, the pickup wins.
  4. Does the group name they gave match one we know?
  5. Does their email address belong to a group we know?
  6. Do we recognise the person who sent it?
  7. None of the above. It goes to a review pile for a human. We never guess, and we never automatically reject.

Why an order, and not a vote

A group using their own dedicated form is a far stronger statement of who's responsible than an email address is. So they don't get equal votes — a weaker clue can never overturn a stronger one.

We do still check the weaker clues, and we note whether they agreed. If they disagree, that's a hint that one of our lists has gone stale, and it goes to whoever looks after the data. It never lands on the dispatcher as a conflict to resolve.

Every clue has three answers, not two

Yes, no, and "we couldn't check". That third one is doing real work: almost a quarter of ride requests reach us with no email address at all, so the email clue often can't be checked rather than checked-and-failed.

Those are completely different facts, and if we recorded them the same way our own records would be misleading us later.

We write down what we checked — not how sure we felt

For every ride we keep a short note: which clue decided it, what that clue actually saw, and how each stronger clue came out. We deliberately do not store a confidence score.

If we want a confidence number later, we can work it out from those notes — and we can change our minds about how to calculate it without having to go back and redo any history.

05What we still have to solve

Five things. The first two are the ones that would actually stop this working.

1. The lists the clues depend on don't existNeeds an owner

Four of the seven clues need a lookup list behind them — which building belongs to which contract, which email domain belongs to which group, which names are the same group spelled differently, which people we already know. None of those lists has been built, and nobody owns building them.

Until they exist, those four clues will honestly answer "couldn't check" every single time, and the whole job falls on the strongest clue alone. The person most likely to have carried this knowledge has left the company.

Blocked on: someone being made responsible for this data

2. Two questions we asked the team and haven't heard back onAwaiting reply

Do some groups share a submission link? A couple appear to. If that's true, then for those groups our strongest clue isn't actually telling us who's responsible, and we need a different answer for them.

Is the state mental-health department one payer or seven? Their billing is changing so that all planned rides go onto a single purchase order, which may change the answer.

Both were emailed on the review thread · no reply recorded

3. We've never written down how to sort the old ridesNot started

Everything above is about rides from here forward. We also have years of past rides that need classifying. The approach exists — it's the same clues in the same order — but it has never been written down, and neither has the plan for actually going back and filling it in.

Ours to write · nothing is blocking it

4. A handful of calls that need a person, not a designNeeds decisions

Can a manager fix a wrong-form ride in place instead of sending it back? If the "can we run it" check fails, is the ride dead or on hold? In Colorado, is it the county the ride goes to, or the county the rider is registered in? How long do we wait for missing paperwork before we bill the agency that sent us the ride? And who owns the list of which facilities count as crisis facilities?

Each one is a short conversation, not a project

5. The payer list needs tidying before it goes to reviewHousekeeping

A few rows have missing or malformed names that the new system won't accept, and our rule for sorting payers into types only manages about three quarters of them. Small, but it should be clean before this goes in front of the architecture review.

Raised in August · nothing done yet

What "finished" would look like

Three things: the old-rides half written down, the who-pays design folded into the main documents once it's approved, and a build list handed to engineering.

The five open items above don't block that package. They're named inside it, so nobody discovers them later as a surprise.
